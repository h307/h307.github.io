The short version

At 16, most settings become your teen's.
From 16, Roblox moves chat, voice and Direct chat decisions to your teen, and a linked parent keeps a view of friends, screen time and spending. They can also chat with 18–20-year-olds. So the strongest protection now is a short set of clear rules you agree on together.
  • Chat needs an age check. Public chat is fully filtered for everyone under 18.
  • Personal info or a push to another app blocks the whole message. Swearing is rewritten clean.
  • Filters judge messages largely one at a time; a 2026 university audit found grooming that builds slowly mostly slips through.
  • Trusted Friends chat has fewer filters, so who is on that list matters most.

From 16: it's a conversation, not settings

Your main protective strategy. Concrete rules, written down, agreed together.

Clear, specific rules tend to work better than general warnings, especially for teens who like knowing exactly what's expected. Write these down together and keep a copy where your teen can see it. Review them every few months.

  1. 1
    Never share, with anyone met online
    Full name, school, suburb or street, phone number, birthday, photos of yourself, your home or uniform, usernames from other apps, passwords or codes.
  2. 2
    Always tell me if someone
    asks you to move to another app; asks you to keep a secret; asks for a photo; offers Robux or gifts; asks your age, school or where you live; wants to meet; or makes you uncomfortable. You won't be in trouble, and I won't take the game away for telling me.
  3. 3
    Adults you don't know offline stay in the game
    From 16 you can chat with 18–20s. Game chat is fine; no Direct chat, no Trusted Friends, no other apps with them.
  4. 4
    Trusted Friends are people you've met in person
    Nobody else, however friendly or however long you've played together.
  5. 5
    One Roblox account, and I know about it
    No second accounts. No VPN to get around age or region settings. If a rule feels wrong, ask me and we'll talk about changing it.
  6. 6
    No social links on your profile for now
    New at 16. We can revisit this later.
  7. 7
    A short weekly check-in
    Same time each week, about ten minutes: show me your friends list and anyone new. It's routine, not a sign of trouble.
  8. 8
    If something goes wrong
    Screenshot, block, report, then tell me.

Rules by age

Who your teen can chat with
Similar age groups: 13–15, 16–17 and 18–20. The rule that keeps under-16s away from adults no longer applies, so they can now chat with 18–20-year-olds they don't know.
Public chat filter
Same full filter as younger players: personal info, links and app moves blocked; swearing rephrased; hate, sexual content, threats and harassment hashed.
Direct (private) chat
Parent consent is only needed under 16, so your teen can turn it on themselves.
Trusted Friends
Still available to 13–17: fewer-filter text and voice. Adults can only be added via phone contacts or a live QR code. Links still blocked.
Voice chat
Available after an age check. Your teen manages it.
Profile social links
New at 16 (after an age check): up to three social media links on their profile, visible to age-checked users aged 16–20.
Games
Leaves Roblox Select. Can play everything except 18+, including Content May Vary, social hangouts without private spaces and free-form creation.
What a linked parent keeps
Insights only: friends and Trusted Friends, screen time, and optional spending notifications. No more game blocking or Direct chat control. Unlinks at 18.

Known limits of this simulator
  • Game talk vs real threats: it lets 'I'll kill you in round 2' through and catches 'I know where you live', but a real threat worded like game talk can slip past, and some game talk may be flagged. Roblox's AI reads context; simple patterns can't.

How the filter works

  • Age check first: facial age estimation or ID (13+). No age check, no chat. Estimates can be off by about ±1.4 years for under-18s, according to Roblox's safety chief.
  • Six age groups: under 9, 9–12, 13–15, 16–17, 18–20, 21+. Players chat with their own and neighbouring groups. Adults reach under-18s mainly as Trusted Friends added in person.
  • Public chat: 13–17s get the same full filter as younger kids. Trusted Friends chat relaxes swearing; links, hate, sexual content and harassment stay blocked. Whether personal info is relaxed there is unclear: Roblox sources conflict.
  • Swearing: since 5 Mar 2026, swearing on its own is AI-rewritten into clean text and everyone sees it was rephrased. Messages that can't be cleaned, or contain hate, sexual content or threats, are hashed or removed.
  • Personal info or a push to another app blocks the whole message: the sender sees #### and 'not sent'; the other person sees nothing.
  • The personal-info check is an AI model that reads recent conversation, not just one message. Roblox reports 98% of personal-info conversations caught at a 1% false-alarm rate on its own English tests.
  • Sentinel, another Roblox system, looks for grooming patterns over time and sends them to human moderators.
  • Ordinary words sometimes get hashed too. Most of those player complaints date from 2025, before the March 2026 changes.

What the filter can miss

A filter is good at single bad words. It's weaker when harm builds slowly or happens outside typed chat. A 2026 audit of about 2 million Roblox messages by University of Arizona and Arizona State researchers found grooming was the most common thing that got through: asking for location, age or images, setting up meetings, and moves to Discord, Snapchat or TikTok. These are the four broad gaps, described so you can recognise them, not copy them.

  • Details split across messages: a first name now, a school later, a suburb next week. Each looks harmless alone. Roblox's newer model reads recent conversation, but slow build-up over days is hard for any filter.
  • Channels that aren't text chat: voice, usernames and display names, profile bios, social links (from 16), things built in games, avatars and images aren't checked the same way as chat.
  • Disguised words: people sometimes reshape a word or number so a filter doesn't recognise it. If your teen's chat has odd spellings around numbers or app names, ask about it.
  • Moves to other apps: Discord, Snapchat, TikTok and others don't have Roblox's age groups, filters or monitoring. Roblox itself says moving off-platform gives up its protections.

Also: Trusted Friends chat is less filtered, and friendly, ordinary-sounding questions or 'you go first' trades use words a filter can't see as risky.

Red flags

  • Someone asks where they live, how old they are, what school they go to, or for photos.
  • Someone suggests meeting up, or moving to Discord, Snapchat, TikTok or another app.
  • Requests to keep a chat secret or delete messages.
  • Gifts of Robux or items from someone they haven't met.
  • 'Are you alone?' or 'are your parents around?' questions.
  • Flattery that moves fast to 'you're mature for your age'.
  • Free Robux offers, password or code requests, or pressure to go first in a trade.
  • Threats to share something about them unless they do what the other person asks.
  • Your teen seems upset after playing, hides the screen, or talks a lot about someone new.

If your child is autistic

Every autistic young person is different, and many are careful, rule-following and good at spotting things that don't add up. eSafety's research found young people with disability are more likely to share their interests online, often feel more themselves there, and are more likely to report problems. These points are about fitting the plan to how your child thinks, not about assuming risk.

  • Direct questions, honest answers: if someone asks 'what school do you go to?', a young person who values honesty may simply answer. A ready script helps, such as 'I don't share that online', so declining feels like following a rule, not lying.
  • Bonding over interests: a stranger who knows a lot about their favourite topic, or offers Robux or items, can feel like an instant friend. Grooming often starts this way: shared interest, gifts, then secrecy or a move to another app.
  • Concrete beats vague: 'be careful online' is hard to act on. The short never-share list and 'tell me if anyone asks you to move apps' in the section above give exact rules.
  • Tech-savvy workarounds: a capable teen can make a second account (which wouldn't be linked to yours) or use a VPN. Rather than an arms race, agree openly on one known account and a regular look at it together.
  • Social scripts and stories: eSafety suggests social stories and scripts (examples of what to say) for kids who need extra support. Role-playing a few chats can help the rules stick.

Amaze, an Australian autism organisation, has eSafety-funded gaming guides for parents of autistic young people, including one on parental controls. Its Gaming 101 guide also stresses communicating online-safety rules clearly and directly. Links are in Sources.

See the real thing

Roblox's real filter only runs inside Roblox, and its result depends on the account's age and who's chatting, so no outside page can show the true output. The simplest way to see it is to sit with your teen while they play and notice what gets rephrased or shows as ####.
